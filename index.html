The Book of Jubilees

R. H. Charles · SPCK, 1917 · reader edition

The Book of Jubilees retells Genesis and the opening of Exodus as a revelation dictated to Moses on Sinai by the angel of the presence, and lays the whole of it on a grid of jubilee periods — forty-nine years each, fifty of them from the creation to the entry into Canaan. This edition follows R. H. Charles’s translation as published by the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge in 1917. The edition’s own 957 notes are set in the margin beside the passages they annotate; the years running down the contents are Charles’s anno mundi reckoning, the book’s own clock.

What is in this edition

The text. R. H. Charles, The Book of Jubilees (London: SPCK, 1917), from the Sacred Texts transcription. Public domain. Charles’s wording, spelling, diacritics and editorial brackets are reproduced as printed; nothing has been modernised.

The margin. Every note in the source is anchored to its point in the text — 957 markers, none orphaned. Notes in gold are editorial corrections made here, not Charles’s.

Corrections made to the source.

  • The section heading at Jub. xvii. 15–18 is printed “(xvi. 15–18)” in the source. Those four verses have been restored to chapter 17, where their content belongs.
  • Jub. xxv. 10 is printed “19.” in the source — a transposed numeral. Renumbered.
  • Six verses lost their numerals in the printing (3:23, 4:6, 13:3, 13:13, 18:2, 22:26). The text is intact; those verses are set with their predecessor and labelled as a range.
  • Charles’s footnotes for printed pages 211–212 — twenty-seven notes across Jub. 49–50, the Passover and Sabbath halakhah — are absent from the source file. Their markers are shown greyed and inert.
  • Jub. 9:2 reads “Sherri” in the source, an OCR corruption of “Shem”; corrected and marked.

Where Charles is now out of date. His introduction dates the book to 109–105 B.C. and assigns it to a Pharisee who supported the Maccabean high priesthood. Both positions have since collapsed. The Qumran finds — thirteen fragmentary Hebrew manuscripts, more copies than of most canonical books — settled the question of the original language that Charles could only argue circumstantially, and pushed the likely date of composition back to roughly 160–150 B.C., in a priestly milieu whose 364-day solar calendar (chapter 6) is the calendar the Qumran community kept. Read the introduction for its philology, not its history.
